The unit math nobody prints

GB vs GiB: your 100 GB disk is 93.1 GiB

Providers advertise decimal gigabytes (1 GB = 1,000,000,000 bytes). Your OS reports gibibytes (1 GiB = 1,073,741,824 bytes). So a "100 GB" SSD shows up as about 93.1 GiB inside the machine. You didn't lose 7 GB — the units changed. Same story for RAM: "8 GB" on the plan page is 7.45 GiB to the OS.

Mbps to MB/s: divide by 8

A 1 Gbps port moves at most ~125 MB/s. A "100 Mbps unmetered" plan caps you at 12.5 MB/s — fine for a VPN, tight for a busy file mirror. When a provider quotes bandwidth in TB per month, convert it to sustained speed:

  • 1 TB/month ≈ 3.1 Mbps sustained (1,000 GB × 8 ÷ 30 ÷ 24 ÷ 3,600)
  • 4 TB/month ≈ 12.3 Mbps sustained — enough for a small video relay, not for 4K streaming to many viewers
  • "Unmetered" usually still has a port-speed cap — read the fair-use clause, not the headline

vCPU: one thread's worth of time

One vCPU is roughly one hyper-thread of a physical core, shared with neighbors. Two kinds exist: dedicated (yours alone, costs more) and shared/burstable (fine for spiky workloads, throttled under sustained load). A $5 plan is almost always shared — great for a blog, bad for 24/7 video encoding.

The $5 trap: three signs your box is too small

  1. Load average stays above your vCPU count. Run uptime: on a 1-vCPU box, a 15-minute load average persistently above 1.0 means the CPU queue never drains.
  2. Swap is in use. free -m showing used swap means RAM ran out and the disk is now pretending to be memory — everything gets 10–100× slower.
  3. OOM kills in the log. dmesg | grep -i oom returning lines means Linux already murdered a process to survive. Step up a tier.

None of these mean the provider is bad. They mean the workload outgrew the tier — the normal, expected reason cheap-VPS buyers upgrade.

Before you commit: the 10-minute verification

  • Ping the test IP. Most budget providers publish one. Over 150 ms to your audience = pick another region.
  • Check the looking glass. It shows real routing from the provider's network — more honest than any "premium network" claim.
  • Start hourly if you can. Providers with per-hour billing let you benchmark for a day for cents before committing to a month.
  • Read the renewal price. The headline is usually an intro rate. The number that matters is what month two costs.

Quick answers

How much RAM does WordPress really need on a VPS?

For under ~10k visits a month, 1 vCPU with 2 GB RAM is the honest minimum — PHP-FPM and MySQL want RAM more than CPU. 1 GB works until plugins pile up, then the site starts swapping and admin pages crawl.

How much RAM per player does a Minecraft server need?

Budget roughly 1 GB of RAM per 5 players. A server for under 10 players wants 2 vCPU with 4 GB RAM — Minecraft is single-threaded and RAM-hungry, so clock speed and memory matter more than core count.

What does "1 TB bandwidth" actually mean?

About 3.1 Mbps of sustained transfer if you spread it evenly across a month (1,000 GB × 8 ÷ 30 ÷ 24 ÷ 3,600). That's plenty for a VPN or a small relay, tight for anything serving video. For bandwidth-heavy workloads, read the TB cap — not the core count — before you buy.

Will my site be slow because it's cheap?

Usually no — cheap plans are slow because they're undersized, not because the provider is bad. Run the three checks above: if load average stays above your vCPU count, swap is in use, or the log shows OOM kills, the workload outgrew the tier and it's time to step up.

Does the price renew higher next month?

Often yes: the headline price is usually an intro rate, and month two is the number that matters. We don't track renewal prices yet (see the "—" column above) — read the provider's terms page before you buy, not the promo banner.

Can I run a VPN on the cheapest plan?

Yes. A WireGuard VPN for 1–3 users runs fine on 1 vCPU with 512 MB–1 GB RAM, typically $3–5/mo. Encryption is cheap — bandwidth allowance and latency to your users matter far more than cores.

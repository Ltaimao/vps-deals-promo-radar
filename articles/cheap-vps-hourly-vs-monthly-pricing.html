The three kinds of cheap numbers on pricing pages

When you scan any cheap VPS price list — ours included — the smallest numbers fall into three buckets:

  1. Hourly (or per-second) rates. Words like "hourly", "per-sec billing", or "usage-based" give these away. DigitalOcean's pricing page advertises per-second billing with a 60-second minimum; Kamatera and Liquid Web show explicit hourly rows. These are built for short-lived workloads, not for leaving a server on all month.
  2. Add-on and per-unit prices. Object storage per GB-month, extra storage slabs (our scrape caught BuyVM's $1.25/month 256 GB storage slab and DigitalOcean's storage lines), bandwidth packs. These ride along with a server; they are not the server.
  3. True monthly plans. "Starting at $1.00" from IONOS, or AlphaVPS's $1.67/month storage VPS. These are the only rows you can compare one-to-one with the $5 or $10 figures in a roundup.

None of the three big roundups we audit (liquidweb.com, hostingadvice.com, lowendbox.com) walk readers through this distinction. Their tables mix "starting at" monthly figures with whatever the provider puts first, which is exactly how a $0.01 row gets misread as a $0.01/month plan.

The 730-hour math

Providers that bill hourly define the conversion against a standard month of roughly 730 hours (24 × 365 ÷ 12 = 730). Vultr's FAQ states its hourly rate is the monthly price divided by 730; the same arithmetic underlies every hourly figure on a pricing page. So:

  • $0.01/hour × 730 hours = $7.30/month if the server never sleeps
  • $0.005/hour × 730 hours = $3.65/month if the server never sleeps
  • Conversely, a $5/month flat plan implies an hourly equivalent of about $0.0068/hour — always-on is the discount, not the markup

Some providers add a monthly cap to hourly billing, so a forgotten instance cannot bill the full 730 hours. Others bill per second with a minimum charge per boot (DigitalOcean's 60-second minimum means a 10-second test still bills 60 seconds). The principle is the same: multiply the hourly rate by the hours you will actually use.

The one decision that matters: always-on vs. sometimes-on

This is the real buying question, and it maps directly to something readers actually ask: is a cheap hourly VPS enough for a staging box, or do I need monthly billing?

Buy monthly if the box runs most of the time. Break-even is simple: hourly rate × hours. At $0.01/hour, 500 hours of uptime costs $5.00 — exactly the price of a typical $5/month flat plan. If your server runs more than roughly 500 hours a month (about 21 days), the monthly plan wins. A blog, a VPN, a game server, a production API: all always-on, all monthly.

Use hourly if the box sleeps. A staging box you spin up for two hours of testing, ten times a month, is 20 hours: $0.20 at $0.01/hour. Compare that with a $5/month plan you leave running out of laziness, and hourly billing is a 25× saving. Hourly billing exists for this job — testing, CI runners, short migrations, "spin it up, break things, destroy it."

Watch the forgotten-server trap. The cheapest hourly plan in the world becomes the most expensive the month you forget to destroy the instance. If you choose hourly, set a reminder to tear the box down — or pick a provider with a monthly cap on hourly billing so there is a ceiling.

How to read our tracker without getting fooled

A few practical rules, using the data on this site as the example:

  • Read the title fragment, not just the number. Our rows keep the provider's own wording. "Hourly", "per-sec", "GiB-month", "Daily/Weekly" tell you the unit; "Starting at" and "Per Month" tell you it is monthly. When the unit is ambiguous — like Hostens' $1.00 "Daily/Weekly" row — treat the number as unconfirmed until you check the provider page.
  • 18 of the 27 priced providers on our tracker have at least one row under $5 — but after separating hourly rates and add-ons, the count of true sub-$5 monthly plans is smaller. We publish the raw rows precisely so you can do this separation yourself; every row carries its fetched date.
  • Never compare an hourly row with a monthly row. "$0.01 vs $5" is not a comparison, it is a category error. Convert first, compare second.

The bottom line

There is no $0.01/month VPS on any reputable price list — there are $0.01/hour rates, storage add-ons, and genuine $1–2/month entry plans, and they are different products for different jobs. Decide whether your server sleeps or not, do the 730-hour math, and the cheapest option stops being a mystery.

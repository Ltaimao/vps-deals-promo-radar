Intro Price vs Flat Rate: The 24-Month Math Behind Cheap VPS Deals

The two pricing camps

Almost every cheap VPS plan falls into one of two camps. Camp one: intro-discount plans. A big headline number for the first term — 12 or 24 months — and a smaller "renews at" number somewhere below it. Hostinger, DreamHost, IONOS, and InMotion (whose 2026-10-08 rows show "Renews at" $16.99–$56.99) all play here. Camp two: flat-rate plans. One number, same every month, no discount clock ticking. DigitalOcean's $4 entry Droplet is the classic example: it bills per second with a 60-second minimum, but the monthly starting price never changes.

Telling the camps apart should be easy, but often isn't. In our 2026-10-08 audit of pricing pages, 23 of 26 providers printed no renewal figure at all — no number to pair with the intro price, just "renews at standard rates" language or nothing. A plan with no visible renewal price is camp one by default; treat it as one.

The 24-month math (do this before you buy)

The formula is simple, and it is the whole method:

Total over your horizon = (intro price × intro months) + (renewal price × remaining months).

Work it through on Hostinger KVM 1. Intro: $6.49 × 24 = $155.76 for the first two years. Then the renewal clock starts: $11.99 × 24 = $287.76 for the next two years. Over four years you pay $443.52 — an effective $9.24/month for a plan advertised at $6.49. The renewal price, not the intro price, is what the plan actually costs you long-term.

DreamHost Stack 4 is steeper: $5.99 × 24 = $143.76 for the first two years, then $14.99 × 24 = $359.76 for the two after — a 2.5x jump, $216 more for the same server. And IONOS shows how the first year itself can mislead: "$5 three times and $14 nine times" is $141 in year one, which is $11.75 a month effective — more than double the $5 headline.

Intro prices move, too. Hostinger's KVM 1 intro rose from $4.99 to $6.49 on 2026-03-03, and its renewal rate rose from $9.99 to $11.99 a few days earlier (2026-02-27), per a 2026 dev.to nightly price-tracking project. Both numbers in the pairing can change — the math only holds if you recheck both.

When the flat plan wins — and when the intro still wins

As one dev.to commenter put it, "flat-rate plans look expensive in month one and cheap in month 30." Run the horizon math and you see why: DigitalOcean's $4 entry costs $96 for 24 months and $192 for 48. Hostinger KVM 1 costs $155.76 then $287.76 — $443.52 over the same 48 months. (Specs differ between the two, so treat this as the method demonstrated, not a buy recommendation: run the same arithmetic on the tier you would actually buy.)

But intro pricing is not a scam — it is a tool with a use case. It wins when your horizon is shorter than the intro term: a 6-month project, a migration you plan to revisit, or a server you fully expect to move when the discount ends. The trap is only buying the intro price for a server you will keep for years. As another dev.to writer warned: "a $1.99 intro that renews at $25.99 is a much more expensive site."

How to find the renewal price (three checks)

  1. Read the small print under the price — the "renews at" line is usually there, in gray, two font sizes down. Hostinger and DreamHost both print it on their pricing pages (that is where our pairings came from).
  2. Search the page for "renew" — InMotion's rows literally say "Renews at" with the dollar figure. If your browser finds nothing, see check three.
  3. Assume camp one and budget the worst case. With 23 of 26 providers printing no renewal number at all (our 2026-10-08 audit), the honest move is to treat a missing renewal price as "at least 2x the intro" — the measured range on real pairings is 1.85x to 2.58x — or pick a flat-rate plan where the number is the number.

Our take

I now price every VPS plan at its renewal rate and treat the intro discount as a rebate, not a price. A $6.49 plan that renews at $11.99 is an $11.99 plan with a two-year coupon — and once you read it that way, the flat $4 or $8 plan stops looking expensive. The cheapest plan over the time you will actually run the server is the cheapest plan. Do the 24-month math; it takes ninety seconds and it has never once told me the headline was the truth.

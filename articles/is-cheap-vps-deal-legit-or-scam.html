Is That $1/Month VPS Deal Legit or a Scam? A 5-Check Vetting Guide

Short answer: run five checks before you pay, and the cheapest deals almost always pass — the scam is rarely the headline price. Here is the 60-second version of the vetting table this guide is built on:

CheckLegit (buy)Scam-shaped (walk away)
1. Renewal pricePrinted next to the headline priceMissing — and in our 2026-10-08 audit, 23 of 26 provider pricing pages showed no renewal number at all
2. Refund window30 days or more, written in the termsNo refunds, or a 3-day window limited to your first service, once per account, ever
3. Price historyStable headline price over weeksHeadline jumps, or renewal multiplies without warning
4. Community footprintReal user reports on Reddit, LowEndTalk, or Web Hosting TalkOnly marketing pages and coupon sites mention it
5. Cheapest-tier fine print$1/mo means $1/mo, billed monthlyThe "$1" row is $0.01/hour, an annual prepay, or a teaser with a locked-in renewal

The data behind this guide: a snapshot of 223 live VPS price cards across 32 providers, taken 2026-10-20, 28 days of tracked price history (190 price series, 116 snapshots), our renewal-price audit from 2026-10-08, and our refund-policy audit from 2026-10-17. Every number below comes from those — nothing is guessed.

Check 1: Is the renewal price printed?

This is the single most predictive check we know. A legit provider shows you what the plan costs after the promotion. A scam-shaped one shows you only the teaser.

In our audit of 26 provider pricing pages on 2026-10-08, 23 showed no renewal price anywhere on the page. That is not an accident — it is the industry's default. When the renewal number is shown, the ratio tells you everything: Hostinger's KVM 1 line is advertised at $6.49/month on a 24-month term and renews at $11.99, a 1.85x jump. DreamHost's plans renew at roughly 2.5x their intro prices. A real user on r/selfhosted watched a bill go up 543% in two years because the renewal math was never on the pricing page.

The rule: if you cannot find the renewal price in one minute of looking, treat the headline price as fiction and budget for 2–3x. A provider that hides the renewal is not necessarily a scam — most of the market does it — but it is a provider that profits from you not asking.

Check 2: How long is the refund window?

The refund window is the provider's bet on its own service. We verified ten providers' official refund pages on 2026-10-17, and the spread is enormous:

Notice the pattern the budget end of the market runs on: the cheaper the headline price, the shorter the exit. One community discussion on LowEndTalk put it bluntly — if it is a cheap VPS, "you are not likely to find a free trial or a money-back guarantee." That is not proof of a scam; it is the standard trade at the bottom of the market. The dangerous combination is annual prepay plus no refund window: you are locked in for 12 months with no exit if the service is bad. Community math says a refund window costs you roughly 3–4x more for the same specs elsewhere — that is the honest price of an exit, and it is worth knowing before you commit.

The rule: first month always monthly. Buy the exit before you buy the deal, and only prepay a year after the provider has proven it does not need your refund.

Check 3: Has the headline price been stable?

Here is where our own tracking helps. We keep 28 days of price history across 190 tracked price series (116 snapshots, 2026-09-23 to 2026-10-20). In that window, seven series crossed the 50% move line — and every one is scrape noise, not a price change. Six are two-snapshot blips from plan-card remapping; the seventh, a DreamHost card, flip-flops between $39.99 and $59.99 as two plan cards swap positions in the scrape. Zero genuine headline-price moves in 28 days. Providers do not normally rug-pull the listed price — the industry norm is a stable headline with the real jump hiding in the renewal column.

What moves is what the price card does not show. The 543% bill increase from r/selfhosted did not come from a headline change; it came from a renewal the pricing page never printed. Independent nightly tracking has caught a 40% increase that a first scrape missed, and a plan whose promotional price ran out after three months while the card still looked the same. So the check is not "did the card price change" — it is "what does the card not price."

The rule: a stable headline price is a good sign but proves nothing about your second invoice. Stability you can verify is worth more than a promotion you cannot.

Check 4: What does the community actually say?

Search "Is [provider] legit or a scam?" — it is one of the most-asked budget VPS questions on Reddit, and the answers are more useful than any review site. Look for three things:

  • Volume over time. A provider with two years of LowEndTalk or r/VPS threads is auditable. A provider that exists only on coupon sites is not.
  • The specific complaint. "Support is slow" is normal for budget hosts. "They deleted my data on cancellation" or "charged my card after I cancelled" is not.
  • The honest middle. The best summary of the budget tier we have seen came from a Hacker News thread: the lowendbox-style hosts "will very likely work, but they don't have the history for me to trust business critical tasks." That is the correct mental model — legit does not mean enterprise-grade.

The rule: no community footprint, no purchase. Marketing copy is a claim; three years of forum threads are evidence.

Check 5: What does the cheapest tier really cost?

The $1 headline is where most "is this a scam?" anxiety lives. Our 2026-10-20 snapshot puts it in context: the median entry plan across 24 providers is $4.77/month; 15 providers start at $5 or less, 7 at $3 or less, and only 3 under $2 — Hostens at $1.00, Latitude.sh at $1.23, BuyVM at $1.25. A genuine $1/month tier is rare, which is why the "$1" rows deserve a second look:

  • Hourly, not monthly. 18 sub-$1 rows in our snapshot, spread across 6 providers, are metered rates ($0.01/hour and similar) — not $1/month plans. They are fine for tests, misleading as headlines.
  • Annual, not monthly. A "$1.83/month" plan may be $21.99 paid up front for the year — and per Check 2, that prepay often comes with a weak refund window.
  • Missing specs. In our audit, 219 price cards produced only 14 that even print the word "KVM". A card that hides the virtualization type, the bandwidth cap, and the renewal price is not lying — it is just not telling you anything you can plan on.

The rule: read the cheapest row as a riddle, not a price. If the billing unit, the term, and the renewal are all clear, a $1 plan is a deal. If any one of them is vague, it is a teaser wearing a price tag.

The 30-second verdict: most cheap deals are legit, the exit is the product

Stack the five checks and a pattern emerges: the budget VPS market is not full of scams — it is full of missing information. Zero of our 223 tracked cards mention refunds. Twenty-three of 26 pricing pages hide the renewal. Eighteen sub-dollar rows look monthly but bill hourly. None of that is fraud; all of it is the provider betting you will not check.

Our take: buy the exit, not the deal. First month always monthly, from a provider whose renewal price is printed and whose refund window you have read. The cheapest honest plan in our snapshot is Hostens at $1.00/month — but the cheapest safe first purchase is the one you can leave. Scams are rare in this market; bad exits are everywhere. Check for the exit first and the price second, and the $1 deals stop looking scary.
